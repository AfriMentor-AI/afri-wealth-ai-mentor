My name is Magatte, and I was born in Senegal on the West Coast of Africa. Oftentimes people ask me, "How did you get into entrepreneurship?" For me, entrepreneurship is on a very personal plane, because other than being an entrepreneur, I have two issues basically that almost keep me haunted.

One of them is the fact that my people are dying at a regular rate. Imagine the workplace, imagine all the seats in this room, and we put young people like you in it. Now let's take all the people in a small fisherman's boat, a pirogue, and we push that boat from the coast where I'm from into the Atlantic Ocean, trying to move up to Europe. As you can imagine, most of those boats don't make it to their destination, and most people end up drowning. Imagine people your age and younger dying every single day because they are trying to reach Europe, hoping to help their family back home, and they end up at the bottom of the ocean serving as fish food.

That's pretty gruesome, isn't it? Well, that's my real story. I have friends, I bring friends who have relatives who are victims on a regular basis. Or a plane gets somewhere in Europe, and all of a sudden a body drops from the sky because somebody squeezed themselves into the landing gear or the cargo section. If they freeze to death up there because of the altitude, when the landing gear opens, they fall off the plane.

That is the sad reality of migration. When people talk about migrants, I have lived with these stories for as long as I can remember having a brain and being able to understand what's going on. In my case, we call them economic migrants. Economic migrants are those people who leave their place of origin, their country, to go to another country in search of jobs.

The second reason that haunts me is the fact that my culture is dying. Why? Because Africans, and people from the developing world in general—people who have been part of dominated cultures—share this dynamic. When people feel that their traditional identity and culture are not respected globally, they tend to push down their own culture while accepting Western culture because it is the accepted, dominant culture.

In my case, I was born in Senegal, raised by my grandmother after my parents moved to France, and after business school in France, I decided to move to the United States. I worked in Silicon Valley during the dot-com heyday, working for companies like Google before most people even knew what those companies were about. What I learned in Silicon Valley created a deep internal crisis for me. Here I was living this amazing life, but every day I opened the newspaper, there was some nasty story about Africa, and a lot of it was related to poverty.

I remember driving down Highway 1 near Big Sur, emotional and crying, and I almost crashed into the ocean. At that moment, I made a deal with God: "From now on, I want everything that I do forward to be related to getting Africa into shape. I don't know what it's going to be, but you have to know that I am committed to it."

Being in Silicon Valley, I learned the magic of entrepreneurship—creating something out of nothing—and developed a deep appreciation for brand philosophy. A brand is a container for culture. I realized that if I wanted my culture to be preserved, it needed to be embedded in products that people all over the world love and respect. Look at brands like Hollister: it's a pure fabrication of a surfer lifestyle, yet young people all around the world buy into that culture.

I decided to use the power of branding to tell the world the story of my culture, get them to love it, and build companies that create high-quality jobs back home so my people don't have to risk their lives crossing the ocean to find work.

My first company, Tiossan, was a beverage company using native Senegalese hibiscus and botanical recipes. My second company, SkinIsSkin, is a skincare company. We practice impact sourcing—buying raw ingredients like shea butter directly from women's cooperatives in Senegal, putting over 4,000 women to work. But we need to go beyond just sourcing raw materials; we need to move up the value chain. Instead of just harvesting raw shea butter for pennies, we must manufacture the finished creams, lotions, and packaged products in Africa. That is where the real value, wealth, and high-paying jobs are created.

When people talk about poverty, poverty is a simple concept: people are poor because they have no money. Why do they have no money? Because they have no income. Where does income come from? A job! Where do jobs come from? Businesses! And where do new jobs come from? Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs) and entrepreneurs!

If we agree that jobs come from entrepreneurs and businesses, shouldn't we focus on making sure there is a good business climate for entrepreneurs to start and grow companies?

Yet when you look at the World Bank's Doing Business index—which measures ten indicators of how easy or difficult it is to start and run a business—almost all African countries sit at the very bottom of the ranking! Almost nobody talks about this, but it is the root cause of systemic poverty.

Let me give you real examples of how bad regulations kill businesses. In the US, if an employee wants to quit, they give two weeks' notice. If a business needs to let an employee go, they give severance or notice. In Senegal, if an employee wants to leave or if you need to lay someone off, the law requires three months' written notice! If they worked for you for six years, you owe them six months of pay. If they worked twelve years, you owe a full year of salary!

Now imagine you are a young person wanting to start a small business. How can you take the risk of hiring five people when firing one underperforming worker could bankrupt your entire company? The result? Nobody hires formally, or businesses refuse to start in the first place!

Another example: in some African countries, the minimum capital required just to open a corporate bank account used to be $1,000! $1,000 is more than the annual income of many citizens! It is a series of absurd, anti-business regulations that choke enterprise at all levels.

And then people wonder why there are no formal jobs, why 80% to 90% of the economy is forced into informal survivalism, why young people get desperate, get on pirogues, and die at sea!

The difference between a young person in America making $15 an hour and a young person in Senegal making $1 an hour for the exact same effort is not talent, ambition, or intelligence. The only difference is the legal and economic framework of the country in which they were born! Poor people live in badly regulated places.

To fix poverty, we must fix the business climate. We need legal reforms that allow local entrepreneurs to flourish.

When it comes to my own business, I practice conscious capitalism and the stakeholder model. Every business decision must be a win-win for six key stakeholders:

Our employees

Our customers

Our suppliers

The environment

The local community

Our shareholders

Some people ask about microfinance and micro-lending platforms like Kiva. Microfinance was a wonderful first step to help people float, but microfinance primarily funds necessity entrepreneurs—people who sell vegetables on the side of the road because there are no jobs. There is a huge difference between a necessity entrepreneur selling fruits to survive, and an opportunity entrepreneur building a manufacturing plant, a tech platform, or a brand that hires hundreds of people. What Africa desperately needs is funding and business climate reforms for the missing middle—the Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs) that drive real economic transformation.

If you care about eradicating poverty, if you care about human dignity, remember that entrepreneurs and enterprise are the true magic that creates prosperity. Do whatever you can to break down the barriers and make it easier for entrepreneurs to do their magic!

Thank you very much.
