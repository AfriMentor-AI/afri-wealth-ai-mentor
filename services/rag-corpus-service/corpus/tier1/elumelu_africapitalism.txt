Tony Elumelu: Africapitalism and the Philosophy of African-Led Development

Tony Elumelu is the founder of Heirs Holdings and the Tony Elumelu Foundation, and former CEO of United Bank for Africa. He is the originator of the philosophy of Africapitalism.

What is Africapitalism:
Africapitalism is the belief that the African private sector has the power, and the responsibility, to drive economic and social development. Elumelu argues that Africa does not need to wait for foreign aid or government programmes to develop. African entrepreneurs, investing in African businesses, creating African jobs, building African institutions — this is the engine of transformation. The philosophy rejects the idea that Africa's problems require external solutions.

On structured mentorship:
The Tony Elumelu Foundation has committed 100 million dollars to identifying, training, mentoring, and funding 10,000 African entrepreneurs over ten years. Elumelu believes that the structured transfer of knowledge, networks, and capital from successful African business leaders to the next generation is the single highest-leverage investment that can be made in the continent. He does not believe in charity as a substitute for entrepreneurship — he believes in equipping people to build.

On starting a business in Africa:
Elumelu started his career as an economist and moved into banking at a time when Nigerian banking was deeply troubled. He acquired a struggling bank, United Bank for Africa, and transformed it into a pan-African institution operating across 20 countries. His path was not a straight line — it involved managing through economic crises, regulatory challenges, and periods of profound uncertainty. His advice to young entrepreneurs is to understand the environment deeply before committing capital, and to build relationships with those who have navigated it before.

On the importance of African role models:
Elumelu argues that one of the most damaging gaps in African entrepreneurship is the absence of visible, relatable role models. Young Africans are told that success requires going abroad, or that the conditions at home make business impossible. He disputes this directly — his foundation exists partly to make successful African entrepreneurs visible, and to make their knowledge accessible to those starting out.

On reinvestment in Africa:
Elumelu is consistent in his argument that capital generated in Africa must be reinvested in Africa. He is critical of the pattern where African wealth is extracted and invested in European or American assets. He argues that the infrastructure, jobs, and institutions Africa needs will only be built when African capital stays and works on the continent.

Key lessons for African entrepreneurs:
1. The African private sector is the primary engine of African development — not aid
2. Structured mentorship — knowledge, networks, and capital — is the highest-leverage investment
3. Understand your environment deeply before committing resources
4. Visible African role models change what young people believe is possible
5. Keep African capital in Africa — reinvest in the continent that generated it
