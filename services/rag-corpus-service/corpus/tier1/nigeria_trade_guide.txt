Starting and Growing a Trade Business in Nigeria: A Practical Guide

Nigeria is Africa's largest economy and most populous country, with over 200 million people and one of the continent's most dynamic informal trading sectors. This guide addresses the practical realities of starting and growing a trade business in the Nigerian context.

Section 1: Choosing Your Trade
The most sustainable trade businesses in Nigeria are built around goods that people need consistently — food staples, clothing, building materials, household goods, and personal care products. The question to ask is not what is fashionable but what is necessary.

Fabric and fashion: Nigeria has a large and growing fashion market. Ankara fabric, lace, aso-oke, and ready-to-wear clothing are consistently in demand. Balogun Market in Lagos, Ariaria Market in Aba, and Kantin Kwari Market in Kano are the major hubs for fabric wholesale. Starting as a retailer buying from these markets and selling in your local area is a proven entry path.

Food trade: Staple foods — rice, beans, garri, yam, palm oil — are always in demand. The supply chain from farm to consumer has many entry points: buying from farmers and selling to processors, buying from processors and selling to retailers, or retailing directly to households. Each step in the chain has a margin.

Electronics and phone accessories: Nigeria's mobile phone market is large and growing. Accessories — phone cases, chargers, earphones, screen protectors — have low unit cost and high turnover. Computer Village in Lagos is the hub for electronics wholesale.

Section 2: Pricing Your Goods
Many traders price by copying competitors without understanding their own costs. This leads to selling at a loss without realising it. Before setting your price, calculate:

Cost of goods: What you paid for the item, including transport to bring it to your location.
Operating costs: Your stall rent, your transport to market, your phone credit for business calls, your packaging — divided across the number of items you sell.
Target profit: What you need to earn above your costs to make the business worthwhile.

Your minimum price is cost of goods plus operating costs. Your selling price must be above this minimum. The difference is your profit margin. Do not let pressure from customers or competitors push your price below your minimum — that is the path to business failure.

Section 3: Managing Stock
Running out of stock costs you sales. Holding too much stock ties up capital and risks spoilage or obsolescence. The balance point — the reorder level — is the stock quantity at which you should place a new order so that it arrives before you run out.

Track your sales daily, even in a simple notebook. After one month, you will know how fast each product sells. Use this data to set your reorder point and your order quantity. This removes guesswork and reduces both stockouts and overstock.

Section 4: The Nigerian Business Environment
Building a business in Nigeria requires resilience and adaptability. Specific challenges include:

Foreign exchange volatility: Many goods in Nigeria are imported or use imported inputs. When the naira depreciates, import costs rise. Successful traders hedge against this by maintaining some savings in stable assets, pricing with a buffer for currency movement, and building supplier relationships that allow flexible payment terms.

Infrastructure: Unreliable power, poor roads, and congested ports add cost and unpredictability to trade. Factor these costs into your pricing. Build relationships with reliable logistics providers. For power-dependent businesses, a small generator or solar inverter is a business necessity, not a luxury.

Regulatory environment: Registration with the Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC) is straightforward and gives your business legal standing. Registration with your local market association or trade union provides protection and access to collective bargaining. Do not operate entirely informally if you intend to grow.

Section 5: Growing Your Business
The pattern of sustainable growth in Nigerian trade is: stabilise, save, expand. Stabilise your current operation — consistent sales, reliable suppliers, satisfied customers. Save a fixed percentage of profit every week — 10 to 20 percent — without touching this reserve. When your reserve equals three months of operating costs, you have a buffer that protects you through difficult periods and funds your next step.

Expansion should be incremental: one new product line, one new location, one new supplier relationship at a time. Each expansion should be funded from savings, not debt. Debt is only appropriate when the expected return clearly and comfortably exceeds the cost of borrowing.
